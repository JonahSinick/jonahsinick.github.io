DRAFT IN PROGRESSEveryone Is Confident. Nobody Can Explain How.

The strange gap at the center of AI timeline forecasting — and what it means for how confident we should actually be.

The Puzzle

The AI forecasting community has converged, remarkably, on a narrow window. Metaculus puts the median for AGI at October 2032. Prediction markets cluster around 2030-2033. The AI Safety Leaders Survey (February 2026) gives a median of 2033 with IQR 2031-2036. Even Yoshua Bengio, previously cautious, now warns of “existential risk within a decade”. The aggressive forecasters — Aschenbrenner, Kokotajlo — say 2027-2030. The skeptics — LeCun, Marcus — argue current approaches won’t get there without fundamental breakthroughs, though neither commits to a specific year. (Even Chollet, who argues the current paradigm is “fatally flawed,” now estimates AGI by ~2030 via a different mechanism.) The spread is maybe 8 years. For a question this hard, that’s striking agreement.

But ask a different question — how do we get there? — and the consensus evaporates. Not because people disagree about the mechanism, but because nobody has one.

We went looking for detailed, mechanistic arguments that explain specifically how the remaining capability gaps close — not trend extrapolation, not “scaling continues and capabilities emerge,” but an actual engineering-level account of how AI goes from “brilliant at tests, unreliable at work” to “reliably replaces a knowledge worker across unstructured tasks” — what we’ll call Broad Cognitive Parity (BCP). Here’s what we found:

Leopold Aschenbrenner (Situational Awareness): Names three drivers — compute, algorithms, “unhobbling” — and counts OOMs. Never explains how any of them close the reliability gap, the open-ended reasoning gap, or the sustained autonomy gap. It’s trend extrapolation organized into categories.

Kokotajlo et al. (AI 2027): A fictional scenario with named agent generations (Agent-0 through Agent-5), each more capable than the last. Gary Marcus’s critique is sharp: the progression is asserted, not explained. Key breakthroughs are invented (“neuralese recurrence”). The scenario reads as plausible fiction, not engineering roadmap. The authors recently pushed their timeline to ~2034 for superintelligence.

Tom Davidson (Epoch AI): A rigorous economic model that explicitly doesn’t model how capabilities improve — just when compute is sufficient, assuming scaling laws hold. Davidson is honest about this: the framework predicts when based on extrapolation, not how based on mechanism.

Dario Amodei (“Machines of Loving Grace”): Describes what happens after AGI. Says nothing about how we get there. Zero mechanistic content on the path.

80,000 Hours (guide): The closest thing to a mechanistic argument we found. Identifies four drivers (scaling, RL reasoning, test-time compute, agent scaffolding) and makes a quantitative point: going from 90% to 99% per-step reliability transforms 20-step task success from 10% to 80%. This is genuinely useful. But it still doesn’t explain why RL will continue producing gains or how scaffolding handles the open-ended reasoning gap.

The pattern: everyone is extrapolating a trend. Nobody is describing a mechanism. The most prominent AI timeline estimates in the world are, at their core, saying: “this exponential curve has held for N years, so it will probably hold for M more years, and when it does, AGI arrives.” That’s the entire argument.

Is that good enough?

When Has This Worked Before?

Technology forecasting has a long history of confident predictions based on trend extrapolation without mechanism. Sometimes it works. Sometimes it fails spectacularly. The distinguishing factor turns out to be informative.

Cases where mechanism-free extrapolation was vindicated

Moore’s Law held for ~50 years despite radical changes in the underlying technology. The semiconductor industry’s ITRS roadmap famously color-coded future nodes: white meant a solution existed, yellow meant one was known, and red meant no known solution. Many nodes were red when first projected. Nobody in 1995 predicted that FinFETs would deliver the 22nm node. Gordon Moore himself said at ISSCC 2003: “No exponential is forever: but ‘Forever’ can be delayed!” — predicting the trend would continue while acknowledging the specific mechanisms were unknown. It continued for another 20+ years through technologies that didn’t exist when he spoke.

Genome sequencing costs fell from $3 billion (Human Genome Project, 2003) to ~$200 today. The “$1,000 genome” goal was first articulated at an NHGRI retreat in December 2001 and formalized through grants in 2004. No one specified the technology. The incumbent (Sanger sequencing) could never have delivered it. Costs tracked Moore’s Law until 2008, then crashed far faster when Illumina’s sequencing-by-synthesis arrived — a technology that didn’t exist when the prediction was made. The forecasters were right on direction and conservative on timeline.

Solar PV costs fell 99% since the 1970s, following a ~20% learning rate per doubling of capacity (Swanson’s Law). Expert forecasts systematically underestimated this: NREL’s 2015 projection for 2050 utility-scale solar was $91/MWh; by 2024 it was $21/MWh — a 77% revision. An MIT study found that the innovations driving cost declines “often originated outside the solar sector” — semiconductor fabrication, metallurgy, glass manufacturing — making the specific mechanism inherently unpredictable. The trend was more predictable than the path.

Cases where mechanism-free extrapolation failed

Fusion power has been “30 years away” since the 1950s. Unlike the success cases, each prediction did specify a mechanism — stellarator, then tokamak, then laser fusion, then ITER — and each mechanism ran into unforeseen physics barriers. A researcher with 60 years in the field summarized: “When the research programs were 10 years old, the reactor was 10 years away. After 20 years, it was 20 years away.” ITER, proposed in 1985, originally targeted first plasma by 2016; it now targets first plasma in 2033-34 and deuterium-tritium operations in 2039, at costs well exceeding the original ~€5 billion estimate.

The pattern

Case Mechanism specified? Prediction correct? Key factor
Moore’s Law No — “red” nodes on roadmap Yes, ~50 years Broad industry with economic incentive; many parallel R&D paths
Genome sequencing No — technology-agnostic goal Yes, beaten Enabling technology didn’t exist at prediction time
Solar PV No — Wright’s Law extrapolation Yes, beaten Cross-industry innovation made mechanism unpredictable
Fusion Yes — specific device each time No, repeatedly Single hard physics problem with unforeseen barriers

The distinguishing factor: mechanism-free extrapolation works when there is a broad technology frontier with many parallel R&D paths and strong economic incentives driving continuous improvement. It fails when success depends on solving a single hard problem where each proposed mechanism runs into unforeseen barriers (fusion).

The critical question for AI: which pattern do the remaining gaps follow?

Our Methodology

We ran a bottom-up assessment with an adversarial structure: a pessimist argued for BCP 2040-2060+, an optimist argued for 2028-2033, neither could see the other’s argument, and two isolated reconciliation agents each saw only one side. Final integration adjudicated disagreements. Full pipeline and supporting analysis are in the appendices.

This process produced a BCP median of ~2034 with wide uncertainty (2029-2046). But the process itself revealed something more interesting than the number: the inability to construct a mechanistic path to that number. Which brings us to the core of this post.

Our Attempt to Build the Missing Argument

We think the absence of a mechanistic argument is itself evidence — mostly for wider uncertainty toward longer timelines. If you can’t describe how something happens, your confidence that it happens on schedule should be lower. So here’s our attempt to construct the argument, with explicit labels on what’s grounded, what’s plausible, and what’s speculative.

The starting point is the key empirical pattern:

Task type Examples AI performance
Bounded, well-specified AIME (100%), GPQA Diamond (94%) At or above human level
Semi-bounded, short SWE-bench Verified (87-94%) Near human level
Open-ended, interactive ARC-AGI-3 (0.37% vs. humans 100%) Far below human level
Sustained, autonomous METR 80% horizon: ~3 hrs (mid-2026) Degrades sharply

AI excels at bounded tasks and struggles at open-ended ones. There are five gaps between current AI and broad cognitive parity. Each needs to close.

Gap 1: Reliability (the error compounding wall)

Current state: 85-95% per-step accuracy. A 10-step workflow succeeds 20-60% of the time. METR 80% time horizon has grown from ~3 minutes (2023) to ~3 hours (mid-2026), doubling roughly every 3-4 months — but still far from multi-day autonomy.

The key insight: You don’t need 99.9% per-step accuracy if you have verification and retry. The error compounding math assumes single-pass forward execution, but real deployment uses multi-pass architectures:

  • Verification + retry: If a system verifies each step with 90% accuracy and retries on detected failure, effective per-step accuracy jumps from 85% to ~97%. Two retry cycles reach ~99.5%.
  • Checkpointing: Roll back to last good state instead of restarting.
  • Modular decomposition: Five agents each doing 10 steps with boundary verification, instead of one agent doing 50 steps.
  • External monitoring: A separate model or human reviews outputs at key decision points.

These are engineering solutions, not research breakthroughs. Companies are building exactly these systems today. The question is whether they’re sufficient — whether engineering can bridge the gap from 85% to effectively 99%+, or whether you need the base model to fundamentally improve.

Assessment: Mostly grounded. If the current doubling rate holds, the 80% horizon reaches ~24 hours by late 2027 without engineering improvements; with verification and retry, possibly sooner. Getting from there to multi-day probably requires model improvement — which brings us back to trend extrapolation. This is the step where engineering yields to hope.

Gap 2: Open-ended reasoning (the ARC-AGI-3 wall)

Current state: 0.37% on ARC-AGI-3 (interactive, no stated goals, requires exploration). 100% for humans.

This is the hardest gap to construct a mechanism for. Three candidate paths:

  • Test-time compute + search: Specialized systems reached ~98% on ARC-AGI-2 via program synthesis and search (though frontier LLMs scored ~24%, with contamination concerns). But ARC-AGI-3 adds interactivity, which breaks pure search — you can’t enumerate possibilities in an environment you have to explore.
  • RL in interactive environments: Training agents through reinforcement learning in games, simulations, and sandboxed environments. Active research direction, not done at frontier scale.
  • Emergent capability from scale: Sufficiently large models might develop interactive reasoning as an emergent capability. Unfalsifiable until it happens or doesn’t.

The Erdos disproof and DeepMind’s Aletheia (63 solutions across 700 open math problems) show genuine autonomous reasoning — but in formal mathematics, the most AI-friendly domain. Whether it transfers to messier domains is exactly the question.

Assessment: Speculative. We can name research directions but can’t point to a specific mechanism that clearly closes this gap. If interactive reasoning proves architectural (requiring world models or learned causal reasoning), timelines extend 5-10 years. Of the five gaps, this is where “we don’t know how” is the most honest answer.

Gap 3: Sustained autonomy

Current state: The METR 80% time horizon is ~3 hours (mid-2026), up from ~3 minutes in 2023. But agents still show goal drift, context saturation, and compounding errors on longer tasks (chanl.ai).

Likely mechanisms:

  • Hierarchical context management: Short-term working memory, medium-term task state, long-term reference store — mimicking how humans manage extended work (we don’t hold 8 hours of raw experience in working memory).
  • Planning and subgoaling: Break multi-day tasks into subtask trees, each short enough to avoid the degradation cliff.
  • Multi-agent architectures: Manager defines subtasks, workers execute in isolated contexts, reviewer checks outputs. No single agent maintains coherence for more than a few hours.

Assessment: Mostly grounded for engineering, uncertain for capability. The 80% horizon is already at ~3 hours and improving rapidly. Whether strategic coherence — not just operational continuity, but judgment quality — maintains over days and weeks is uncertain. This probably requires improvements in metacognition (Gap 4).

Gap 4: Metacognition (knowing what you don’t know)

Current state: MIRROR benchmark shows severe compositional calibration failures — models have some domain-specific self-knowledge but cannot reliably combine it across domains (arXiv:2604.19809).

Why it matters: Metacognition relaxes the error compounding constraint. Humans don’t achieve 99.9% per-step accuracy — we achieve ~90-95% — but we notice when we’re unsure and seek help.

Candidate mechanisms:

  • Explicit calibration training: RLHF with calibration-specific rewards. Reward “I’m not sure” when accuracy is genuinely low.
  • Ensemble self-consistency: Multiple independent responses; agreement measures confidence.
  • Probing internal activations: Internal representations often contain better calibration information than verbalized confidence (Anthropic interpretability research).
  • Separate critic models: Dedicated evaluation models that don’t need to understand their own limitations.

Assessment: Partially grounded. External metacognition (ensemble, critics) is engineering and works today. Internal metacognition may require training innovations. External methods probably get 70-80% of the value, but the remaining gap matters for truly autonomous long-horizon work.

Gap 5: Cross-domain generalization

Current state: Dramatic progress in coding, but METR notes tasks are “primarily software engineering, machine learning, or cybersecurity”.

Why coding might be special: Code has verifiable outputs (compiles or doesn’t), fast feedback loops (run and see), compositional structure (functions call functions), and full-text knowledge bases (documentation). Most knowledge work lacks these properties. Legal reasoning has ambiguous correctness. Medical diagnosis has slow, noisy feedback. Management has no compiler.

Candidate mechanisms:

  • Domain-specific RL environments: Build training environments for non-coding domains. Labor-intensive but straightforward.
  • Transfer through general reasoning: If models develop general planning, decomposition, and verification skills, these should transfer. The Erdos disproof suggests some general reasoning exists — the question is how much.
  • Economic pull: Revenue from coding funds expansion into adjacent domains. Each successful domain provides training data for the next.
  • Human-AI collaboration as bridge: AI as powerful assistant (human reviews), gradually taking over as domain-specific capability matures.

Assessment: Uncertain. The economic pull mechanism is grounded and will happen regardless. General reasoning transfer is plausible but undemonstrated. We think generalization happens domain-by-domain over 3-5 years. This is the primary driver of the gap between current capabilities and BCP — not a single hard problem, but the slow process of expanding from coding to everything else.

The timeline of mechanisms

Period What happens Grounding
2026-2027 Engineering reliability: verification + retry, decomposition, multi-agent. 80% horizon → 2-8 hours. Grounded — techniques exist in prototype
2027-2028 Scaling: 5e27-1e28 FLOP runs. Most bounded benchmarks saturated. External metacognition standard. Mostly grounded — requires scaling trajectory to hold
2028-2030 AI-assisted research produces real algorithmic improvements. Multi-day autonomy in coding + 2-3 structured domains. Partially speculative — depends on Erdos-type results generalizing
2030-2032 Domain-by-domain expansion: coding → data science → document analysis → research → consulting. Plausible — follows economic pull, pace uncertain
2032-2035 Unstructured domains: strategic analysis, creative work, novel research. Multi-week reliability. Speculative — hardest domains, least clear path

So Which Historical Pattern Does AI Follow?

This is the crux. The answer is: different gaps follow different patterns.

The reliability gap is somewhere in between. Engineering solutions (verification, retry, decomposition) are grounded and will deliver meaningful gains — more like Moore’s Law. But closing the last mile of reliability (from 99% to 99.9%+ per-step) may require model-level improvements that we can’t describe mechanistically.

Open-ended reasoning might follow the fusion pattern. The ARC-AGI-3 gap (0.37% vs. 100%) is qualitatively different from benchmark performance — it requires interactive exploration, implicit goal inference, and real-time adaptation. If this is a qualitative barrier rather than a quantitative scaling problem, trend extrapolation is unreliable and timelines could be much longer. Specialized systems reached ~98% on ARC-AGI-2 via program synthesis, but frontier LLMs scored only ~24% (with contamination concerns), and ARC-AGI-3’s addition of interactivity may represent the qualitative barrier.

Sustained autonomy and metacognition are primarily constrained by reliability and open-ended reasoning — their trajectories depend on those gaps rather than following an independent pattern.

Cross-domain generalization follows the solar panel pattern — driven by economic pull and cross-domain innovation, with the specific path inherently unpredictable. This is the most optimistic analogy for the five gaps, suggesting generalization will happen but through mechanisms we can’t currently foresee.

The implication: our confidence in BCP should be lower than most forecasters assume, because the BCP-critical gaps include at least one (open-ended reasoning) that may follow the “hard problem” rather than “broad frontier” pattern. The bounded-task progress that drives most forecasters’ confidence follows the Moore’s Law pattern — broad frontier, many R&D paths, strong economic pull. But that progress isn’t the bottleneck for BCP.

The String Theory Precedent

The analysis above suggests that the evidence for short BCP timelines is weaker than it appears — the hardest gaps lack mechanistic paths. But a natural objection: the smartest people in AI converge on short timelines. Doesn’t that convergence itself constitute evidence?

The scope of the analogy we’re about to draw is narrow but precise. Unlike string theory, AI is an empirical field with empirical results — models scoring 100% on AIME and 94% on GPQA Diamond are real, measured, replicated achievements. Nobody disputes that current AI methods work. The question is whether they will resolve the central open problem: closing the gap from bounded-task excellence to broad cognitive parity. That is structurally the same question the naturalness program faced — will these methods solve the hard problem? — and convergent expert confidence turned out to be a poor guide.

What string theorists predicted

For 30+ years, the “naturalness” program in theoretical physics made a specific, testable prediction: supersymmetric particles must exist near the electroweak scale (~1 TeV) to solve the hierarchy problem without fine-tuning. This was not a fringe view. It was endorsed by the leading minds in the field:

  • Edward Witten (widely considered the greatest living theoretical physicist) predicted string theory would “dominate physics for the next 50 years” (mid-1980s) and said “many physicists do suspect that our present decade is the decade when supersymmetry will be discovered.”
  • Brian Greene discussed superpartners, fractionally charged particles, and cosmic strings as “smoking gun” experimental signatures in The Elegant Universe (1999). Twenty-five years later: none found.
  • Gordon Kane predicted superpartners at LEP energies (~100 GeV), then at the Tevatron (~1 TeV), then at the LHC (~several TeV), then beyond the LHC — textbook goalpost-moving across 20+ years.
  • Maria Spiropulu (Caltech experimentalist, 2016): “If you ask people of my generation, we were almost taught that supersymmetry is there even if we haven’t discovered it.”

What actually happened

The LHC found the Higgs boson in 2012 — and nothing else. No superpartners. No extra dimensions. No micro black holes. No fractionally charged particles. No cosmic strings. The “nightmare scenario” — finding exactly the Standard Model and nothing beyond it — came true.

Nima Arkani-Hamed, the leading theorist of the naturalness program, acknowledged in 2016: “It’s striking that we’ve thought about these things for 30 years and we have not made one correct prediction that they have seen.”

David Gross (Nobel laureate) said at the 2005 Solvay Conference: “We don’t know what we are talking about” — comparing the field’s state to 1911, when physicists were “missing something absolutely fundamental.”

In 2017, Gian Francesco Giudice formally declared “the dawn of the post-naturalness era” — acknowledging that the guiding principle of beyond-Standard-Model physics for 30+ years had failed.

How did this happen?

The mechanisms are specific and identifiable:

Beauty-as-evidence. Mathematical elegance was treated as empirical confirmation. Witten: “I just think too many nice things have happened in string theory for it to be all wrong.” He argued that any sufficiently advanced alien civilization would independently discover string theory — not as a human artifact but as a feature of reality. Sabine Hossenfelder documented this in Lost in Math (2018): “The belief in beauty has become so dogmatic that it now conflicts with scientific objectivity.”

Unfalsifiable core with falsifiable periphery. String theory itself makes no specific low-energy predictions. Supersymmetry at the LHC was a separate prediction that string theory merely “motivated.” When SUSY failed, string theorists could retreat to “string theory doesn’t actually predict low-energy SUSY.” This structure makes the core theory immune to any experimental result — exactly the feature Feynman identified in 1987: “I don’t like that for anything that disagrees with an experiment, they cook up an explanation.”

Goalpost shifting. Kane predicted SUSY at LEP → Tevatron → LHC → beyond LHC. Each null result led to raising the predicted mass scale rather than updating on the theory. The predictions were always just beyond the current experimental frontier.

Institutional lock-in. String theory hiring peaked around 1999, when more string theorists were hired than all other particle theory specialties combined at elite departments. Young physicists faced career pressure to work on string theory or risk marginalization. The community trained the next generation in its own framework.

The “only game in town” defense. When pressed on predictive failures, string theory was defended as the best available approach to quantum gravity — regardless of whether it was actually correct. Being the best available option is not evidence of correctness.

The AI parallel

The structural parallels are specific:

String Theory AI Timelines
Scaling laws in physics (naturalness, hierarchy) are mathematically elegant and predictive in-sample Scaling laws in AI (loss curves, benchmark trajectories) are mathematically elegant and predictive in-sample
Beauty/elegance treated as evidence that the theory describes reality Clean exponential trends treated as evidence that AGI is near
Unfalsifiable core (“string theory is correct”) with falsifiable periphery (“SUSY at 1 TeV”) Unfalsifiable core (“scaling leads to AGI”) with falsifiable periphery (“Humanity’s Last Exam >80% by 2028”)
Goalpost shifting when predictions fail (raise the energy scale) Potential for goalpost shifting when benchmarks plateau (redefine AGI, cite new benchmarks)
The smartest people in the field converge → assumed to be evidence of correctness The smartest people in AI converge → assumed to be evidence of correctness

Institutional incentives align with the paradigm (hiring, grants, prestige) Financial and career incentives align with short timelines (valuations, funding, status)

And there’s an even more direct precedent: the founders of AI themselves. Herbert Simon predicted in 1965 that “machines will be capable, within twenty years, of doing any work a man can do.” Marvin Minsky predicted in 1970 that a machine with “the general intelligence of an average human being” could be built within 3-8 years. These were not cranks — they were among the most brilliant computer scientists who ever lived, with specific technical reasons for optimism. They were wrong for 60+ years. The current generation’s response is “but we have fundamentally better tools (deep learning, transformers, scaling)” — which is exactly what each previous generation said about their tools (symbolic reasoning, expert systems, neural networks).

None of this proves current AI timeline estimates are wrong. It shows that convergent confidence among brilliant people, in the absence of a mechanistic path to the goal, is not reliable evidence by itself — even when the people are genuinely brilliant and the framework is genuinely productive. The question is not “do smart people agree?” but “can anyone describe the specific mechanism by which the remaining gaps close?” For BCP, as the analysis above shows, the answer is: only partially.

What We Actually Estimate

The question that matters is: when does AI achieve BCP — not just ace tests, but actually do the job? AI is already superhuman on most bounded cognitive tasks (math competitions, science knowledge, coding benchmarks), but the bounded/open-ended pattern means test performance doesn’t predict job performance.

The binary structure

Before giving numbers, we want to flag something the analysis revealed that we think is more important than the specific estimates.

The epistemic situation is more binary than a smooth probability distribution suggests. Either the current paradigm (transformer scaling + RL + scaffolding) is sufficient for BCP — in which case the debate is about when between ~2029-2038 — or it isn’t, in which case we need a fundamentally new approach, and we have almost no basis for estimating how long that takes.

The Simon and Minsky predictions discussed above illustrate this concretely. They weren’t wrong about the timeline within their paradigm — symbolic AI really did hit a wall roughly when critics expected. They were wrong about how long it would take to find the next paradigm. It wasn’t “add 10 years” — it was multiple decades and multiple paradigm shifts (expert systems, neural networks, deep learning, transformers) before the field reached its current capabilities. The within-paradigm error was 5-10 years. The cross-paradigm error was 50+.

One important disanalogy: a researcher in 2032 hitting a wall with transformers will have AI tools that Minsky never had. Current AI is already powerful enough to meaningfully accelerate research into fundamentally different architectures. This compresses the right tail relative to historical base rates — but by how much is unknowable.

This means any BCP estimate is really two estimates stapled together: a within-paradigm estimate (which can be reasoned about mechanistically) and a cross-paradigm estimate (which is largely a guess). Most forecasters are implicitly betting on the within-paradigm scenario. The question is how much weight the cross-paradigm scenario deserves.

Wide uncertainty is the honest answer. The mechanistic argument is grounded through ~2030 (engineering + scaling) and speculative after that. We assign ~24% probability to a scenario where current architectures prove insufficient and a fundamentally new approach is needed (BCP ~2038-2045). But given the binary structure above, this range is anchored by proximity to the median rather than any independent reasoning about how long paradigm shifts take. The 90th percentile could reasonably be in the 2050s, and the analysis’s own central claim — that nobody can describe the mechanism for closing the hardest gaps — cuts against confident upper bounds as much as it cuts against confident lower bounds.

Why not sooner?

AI already crushes most benchmarks. Why ~2034 instead of ~2028? Three reasons:

  1. The reliability constraint: The binding constraint isn’t “can AI do X?” but “can AI do X reliably enough for autonomous deployment?” The METR 80% time horizon has reached ~3 hours (mid-2026) and is doubling every 3-4 months — impressive, but multi-day sustained work is still orders of magnitude away.
  2. The generalization question: Progress in coding doesn’t straightforwardly predict progress in domains without verifiable outputs and fast feedback.
  3. The bounded/open-ended boundary: AI handles bounded tasks at or above human level. What remains — open-ended reasoning, sustained autonomy, novel problem-solving — is where progress is slowest and the gap widest.

Conflicts of Interest — Applied Symmetrically

Nearly every prominent forecaster has financial stakes:

Incentivized toward shorter timelines: Lab CEOs (Amodei, Altman, Hassabis) — valuations and fundraising. AI investors (Aschenbrenner’s $5.5B fund, Schmidt) — portfolio returns. Hardware vendors (Huang) — market cap.

Incentivized toward longer timelines: Academic researchers (76% of a 2025 AAAI survey said scaling current approaches is unlikely to produce general intelligence) — funding depends on “new paradigm needed.” Alternative-architecture founders (LeCun/AMI Labs with $1.03B, Sutskever/SSI valued at $32B) — company valuations depend on current approaches being insufficient. ARC Prize creator (Chollet) — benchmark relevance depends on current architectures not solving it via scaling alone.

Least conflicted: Prediction markets, superforecaster surveys, Epoch AI empirical tracking. These cluster around BCP ~2030-2035.

We applied this analysis symmetrically throughout. When a lab CEO says “AGI by 2027,” we discount it. When an academic says “scaling can’t work,” we discount that too.

Falsifiable Predictions

Seven predictions that discriminate between timelines.

1. METR 80% Time Horizon (Dec 2027)

Threshold: >24 hours. Confidence: 40%. The 80% horizon is ~3 hours as of mid-2026, doubling every 3-4 months. If the doubling rate holds, this threshold is met by extrapolation — but sustained doublings are the question, not the assumption. If the horizon plateaus below ~8 hours, it suggests the reliability gap resists scaling. Source: METR

2. ARC-AGI-3 Progress (Dec 2027)

Threshold: Best model >10%. Confidence: 30%. If met: bounded/open-ended boundary is permeable — strongest signal for the Moore’s Law pattern over the fusion pattern. Source: ARC Prize

3. Non-Mathematical Scientific Discovery (Jun 2028)

Threshold: AI autonomously produces a verified, published result in biology, chemistry, materials science, or ML. Confidence: 35%. If met: Erdos/Aletheia generalizes beyond formal math.

4. Enterprise AI Success — Non-Coding (mid-2028)

Threshold: >35% success rate past pilot to production. Confidence: 20%. If met: deployment gap narrowing beyond coding. Source: Gartner/McKinsey surveys

5. Humanity’s Last Exam Saturation (Dec 2028)

Threshold: Best model >80%. Confidence: 45%. If met: scaling continues on hardest bounded tasks. Source: HLE leaderboard

6. Hyperscaler Capex (Q1 2028)

Threshold: YoY growth >25%. Confidence: 55%. If met: compute buildout sustains. Source: earnings reports, Epoch AI

7. Hallucination Rate (Dec 2028)

Threshold: Best model <2% on SimpleQA (a factual accuracy benchmark measuring hallucination rate on verifiable questions). Confidence: 35%. If met: hallucination reducible without architectural change.

If the 2027 predictions mostly favor shorter timelines, BCP median should shift to ~2031. If longer, to ~2037+.

The Bottom Line

BCP by ~2034 has half a mechanistic argument and half a hope. The first half — engineering reliability gains and scaling through ~2030 — is grounded. The second half — open-ended reasoning, cross-domain generalization, sustained autonomy in unstructured domains — requires breakthroughs we can name but can’t describe. This is where honest uncertainty lives, and it’s wider than most forecasters acknowledge.

The right tail matters more than you think. The ~24% probability we assign to BCP after 2040 reflects the possibility that the open-ended reasoning gap follows the fusion pattern (qualitative barrier) rather than the Moore’s Law pattern (quantitative frontier). Nobody — including us — can currently distinguish between these patterns with confidence. If ARC-AGI-3 stagnates below 5% through 2027 while bounded benchmarks continue saturating, that’s strong evidence for the qualitative barrier interpretation, and BCP timelines should extend significantly.

The string theory lesson applies. The current AI forecasting consensus shares specific structural features with the naturalness program in physics: mathematical elegance treated as empirical evidence, unfalsifiable core claims with falsifiable peripheral predictions, institutional incentives aligned with the paradigm, and convergent confidence among brilliant people driven by shared priors rather than independent evidence. None of this proves the consensus is wrong — but it proves that convergent confidence among smart people is not, by itself, reliable evidence of correctness. The question to ask is not “do smart people agree?” but “can anyone describe the specific mechanism?” For BCP, the honest answer is: only partially.

The most valuable thing about constructing the mechanistic argument isn’t the argument itself — it’s what the attempt reveals about where our knowledge runs out. For bounded tasks (test performance, benchmark scores), knowledge runs out around the details. For BCP, knowledge runs out around the fundamentals — whether the remaining gaps are quantitative or qualitative. That difference should be reflected in our confidence levels, and for most forecasters, it isn’t.
